Protein

Training tells muscle to grow; protein supplies the building blocks. Older muscles need a bit more protein at a time to respond fully, so how you spread it across the day matters too.

How much a day?

AdviceAmountWho it's for
Australian 46 g a day (19 to 70)
57 g a day (over 70)		Enough for most healthy people. Not set with building muscle in mind.
PROT-AGE and ESPEN expert groups 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg
1.2 g per kg or more if active		Older adults, to keep muscle and function. More (1.2 to 1.5 g per kg) during illness, except severe kidney disease.
Trials of training plus protein Benefit levelled off at about 1.6 g per kgAdults doing resistance training; extra protein added a little strength and lean mass, less in older people.

For a 70 kg woman, 1.2 g per kg is 84 g of protein a day: nearly twice the recommended dietary intake of 46 g.

Protein at each meal

Spread it across your meals

  • In laboratory studies, healthy men around 71 needed about 0.4 g of protein per kg of body weight in a single meal to switch on fully, compared with 0.24 g per kg in men around 22. For a 70 kg woman, 0.4 g per kg is about 28 g. Women weren't studied.
  • In a small study, eating about 30 g of protein at each of three meals gave 25% more muscle protein synthesis over the day than the same total eaten mostly at dinner.
  • If most of your protein comes at dinner, adding a protein food to breakfast is often the easiest change to make.
  • Eating protein right before or after a workout made no difference once total daily protein was taken into account. Hitting your daily total matters more than timing.

Which protein?

  • Leucine-rich foods help. In 22 women aged about 69, a small dose of milk protein with added built muscle protein as well as a larger dose of whey. Dairy, meat, fish, eggs and soy are good sources.
  • Plant protein works too. Soy and animal protein supplements gave similar gains in strength and lean mass with resistance training. If you eat mostly plants, combine legumes, soy foods, nuts and seeds across the day.
  • Higher protein is kind to healthy kidneys. Diets with 1.5 g per kg a day or more didn't harm kidney function in adults without kidney disease. If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor or dietitian first.

What a standard serve looks like

The Australian Dietary Guidelines define one serve of lean meat, fish, eggs, tofu, nuts, seeds or legumes as any of these:

65 g cooked lean red meat80 g cooked chicken or turkey100 g cooked fish, or a small can2 large eggs1 cup (150 g) cooked lentils, chickpeas or beans170 g tofu30 g nuts or seeds

Milk, yoghurt and cheese are a separate food group and add protein too. Protein per serve varies between foods and brands, so check the nutrition panel when you can.

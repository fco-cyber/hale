    Understand

    Muscle after 40

    Muscle is the engine for everything from opening a jar to catching yourself on a stair. Here is what changes with age, why strength matters more than size, and why the picture is a hopeful one.

    What muscle is made of

    Musclemade of many bundles
    a bundle of fibres
    Muscle fibreone long muscle cell
    a chain of

    Each muscle is built from bundles called fascicles. Each fascicle holds many long muscle fibres, and each fibre is packed with myofibrils: long chains of sarcomeres. When a nerve signal arrives, the sarcomeres shorten together and the muscle pulls on its tendon.

    Strength training makes fibres thicker by adding more of these contracting proteins, which is called . It also trains your nervous system to switch on more of the muscle at once, which is part of why you feel stronger within weeks, before your muscles look any different.

    Strength falls faster than size

    Muscle size and strength both decline with age, but not at the same pace. In a review of studies across adulthood, women aged 75 lost about 0.64% to 0.70% of their muscle mass a year, and about 2.5% to 3% of their strength. In the Health ABC study of adults aged 70 to 79, leg strength fell about three times faster than leg muscle, and people who kept or even gained muscle still lost strength. That points to a fall in muscle quality: how well each kilogram of muscle works.

    Why strength matters for health

    • In 2,292 adults aged 70 to 79, weaker grip and leg strength were linked with higher mortality, but muscle size was not.
    • In nearly 140,000 adults aged 35 to 70 across 17 countries, weaker grip went with higher death rates from any cause and from heart disease. Grip strength is a simple, inexpensive way to estimate risk.
    • Adults who did muscle-strengthening activities had a 10% to 17% lower risk of death, heart disease, cancer, diabetes and lung cancer, separately from their aerobic exercise.

    These are . They show links, not proof that getting stronger lengthens life: people who are stronger may also be healthier in other ways. The trials further down show what training itself changes.

    Sarcopenia: when muscle loss becomes a health problem

    means losing muscle strength together with muscle mass or quality. The European consensus, which Australian and New Zealand guidelines adopted in 2022, puts low strength first as its key sign. A 2024 global definition agreed that muscle mass, muscle strength and strength for a given amount of muscle are all part of it.

    The hopeful part: muscle responds at any age

    • A Cochrane review of 121 randomised trials with 6,700 older adults found that progressive resistance training had a large effect on strength and a moderate to large effect on getting up from a chair, improved overall physical ability a little, and eased pain in people with osteoarthritis. Serious harms were rare.
    • In adults over 50, resistance training increased strength by 24% to 33% on average, with bigger gains from higher-intensity training. It added an average of 1.1 kg of , more with higher-volume programmes.
    • In trials of healthy adults averaging 65 and over, training improved strength substantially and muscle size by a smaller amount.
    • Young and middle-aged women and men given the same programme gained muscle size and leg strength to a similar degree, and women gained more upper-body strength relative to where they started.

    Common worries, and what the evidence says

    "Lifting weights will make me bulky."Muscle builds slowly

    In trials of adults over 50, resistance training programmes added about 1.1 kg of lean mass on average. That is enough to make a real difference to strength and function, and far from a bodybuilder's physique.

    "Light weights tone, heavy weights bulk."Both build muscle

    When sets are taken close to the point where you can't do another good repetition, light and heavy loads build muscle size about equally. Heavier loads build more maximal strength. So you can choose loads that suit your joints and your confidence, and gradually include some heavier work.

    "It's too late for me to start."It isn't

    Trials in older adults show clear gains in strength, and in everyday tasks such as getting up from a chair, from progressive resistance training.

    "I walk every day. Isn't that enough?"Walking and strength training do different jobs

    Walking is excellent: more daily steps are linked with lower mortality, levelling off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults over 60. But muscle-strengthening activity was linked with lower risk separately from aerobic activity, and doing both was linked with the lowest risk. That is why guidelines ask for both.

    Menopause and muscle

    The years around the last period change how the body stores fat, keeps muscle and holds on to bone. Here is what large studies of women have found, and what does and doesn't help.

    What changes around the final period

    The US Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN) has followed thousands of women through the , measuring body composition and bone density with year after year. Its findings are lined up below against the .

    • In SWAN, the rate of fat gain doubled at the start of the transition while lean mass declined, but overall weight did not climb any faster. So the scales can hide a real shift from muscle towards fat.
    • In the Finnish ERMA study of 234 women aged 47 to 55, lean mass, leg lean mass and thigh muscle size all fell between perimenopause and early postmenopause. Menopausal status predicted every muscle measure, and physical activity helped explain which women kept more muscle.
    • Over 10 years, women in SWAN lost on average 10.6% of their spine bone density and 9.1% at the hip, most of it around the transition. More on bones

    Does hormone therapy protect muscle?

    Strength

    A small effect

    In 23 studies of postmenopausal women, gave about 5% more strength, mostly in small hand muscles.

    Muscle mass

    No clear effect

    Pooled randomised trials in women 50 and over found hormone therapy neither helped nor harmed lean body mass. The quality of evidence was low.

    Hormone therapy is mainly prescribed for symptoms such as hot flushes, and the decision depends on your own health history, so it's one to make with your doctor. On current evidence it isn't a way to keep muscle: training is. Find a menopause doctor

    Hot flushes and exercise

    A Cochrane review found too little evidence to say whether exercise in general eases hot flushes and night sweats. One randomised trial is more encouraging for strength training specifically: in 65 women averaging 55 with frequent hot flushes, 15 weeks of strength training three times a week cut moderate to severe flushes by 44%, compared with 2% in women who didn't change their activity. One trial isn't enough to be sure.

    What this means for you

    • Start now, whatever stage you're at. Muscle responds to training at every age studied, including long after menopause.
    • Train for strength on two or more days a week. How to train
    • Eat enough protein, spread across meals. Protein
    • Look after your pelvic floor. Pelvic floor training works for leaking, and lifting weights hasn't been linked with more prolapse symptoms. Pelvic floor and lifting

    Mind and motivation

    Strength training changes more than muscle. And for most people the hard part isn't the exercise itself: it's starting, and keeping going when life gets busy.

    Mood, sleep and wellbeing

    Mood

    Less depression

    Across 33 randomised trials with 1,877 people, resistance training reduced depressive symptoms by a moderate amount, whatever people's health and however much they trained.

    Sleep

    Better sleep quality

    In 13 studies, regular resistance exercise improved sleep, most of all sleep quality, along with anxiety and low mood. A single session had inconsistent effects.

    Hot flushes

    Possibly fewer

    One trial found 15 weeks of strength training cut moderate to severe hot flushes by 44%. Promising, but not yet confirmed.

    If your mood has been low for more than a couple of weeks, or you've lost interest in things you usually enjoy, please talk to your GP. Exercise can be part of feeling better, alongside other support.

    How it feels as you begin

    Some muscle soreness is normal. A day or two after a new exercise, muscles can feel stiff and tender. It settles within a few days and becomes much milder as your body adapts. Start with fewer sets than you think you can do, and it will be gentler.

    Strength comes before shape. You will usually notice things getting easier (stairs, shopping bags, getting off the floor) weeks before you see any change in the mirror. Your nervous system learns quickly; muscle builds more slowly.

    Confidence follows competence. Many women feel unsure in a gym, or worry about hurting themselves. Learning a few movements well, and seeing your numbers climb in a log, builds a different kind of confidence: trust in your own body.

    Making it stick

    • Book it in. Put two sessions a week in your diary like appointments.
    • Attach it to something you already do, such as after your morning coffee or before the evening news.
    • Start small. Two 20-minute sessions a week is a real start: the biggest drop in risk in the cohort studies came at about 30 to 60 minutes of muscle-strengthening a week.
    • Get some coaching. Supervised training improved strength and balance more than training alone in adults over 65. A few sessions with an or physiotherapist can set you up well.
    • Write it down. A log turns effort into visible progress. Use the session log
    • Train with someone, or join a class for women or older adults, if company helps you turn up.

    Common barriers, and ways round them

    "I don't have time."Small doses count

    When the weekly amount is the same, training a muscle once, twice or three times a week gives similar growth, so you can fit sessions where they suit you. A 20-minute full-body session of four to six exercises is enough to start.

    "Gyms feel intimidating."Home works too

    Muscle grows with light or heavy loads when sets are taken close to effort, so body weight, bands and a pair of dumbbells at home can do a lot. The programme builder has a home option.

    "I'm worried about hurting myself."Start low, progress slowly

    In trials of older adults, aches and muscle soreness were common, but serious harms from resistance training were rare. Do the pre-exercise check-in, learn each movement with light loads, and add load gradually.

    "I'm exhausted, flushing and sleeping badly."Keep a minimum

    On rough days, do a shorter version: one set of each exercise, or just your leg exercises. Regular training may itself improve sleep quality over time.

    "I'm not seeing results."Check the basics

    Look at your log: are the weights or repetitions creeping up every week or two? If not, add a little. Make sure sets finish with only a few good repetitions left in reserve, and that you eat enough protein. How to progress

    Train

    How to train

    You don't need a complicated programme. Five principles, applied steadily for months, sum up what trials in women and men over 50 have found works.

    1. 1. Work hard enough, without going to exhaustion

      Finish each when you could do only one to three more good , called . Going all the way to failure gave no extra gain in strength or muscle in pooled trials. With sets taken this close to effort, light and heavy loads build muscle size about equally.

    2. 2. Progress gradually

      Muscles adapt to what you ask of them, so ask a little more over time: more repetitions, then more weight. This is called , and programmes that built up to higher intensities produced bigger strength gains in adults over 50.

    3. 3. Do enough sets

      More weekly sets per muscle led to more growth, in a graded way. In trials of healthy older adults, two to three sets of seven to nine repetitions per exercise worked best for strength. Start with one or two sets and build up.

    4. 4. Train two or three days a week

      Guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening on two or more days a week. When the weekly total is the same, how you split it across days makes little difference, so choose what fits your life.

    5. 5. Over time, add some heavier and quicker work

      Heavier loads build more maximal strength than light ones. Lifting briskly builds , which is linked with everyday function in older people, and power training improved function. Lift with intent, lower with control.

    How hard is hard enough?

    A simple way to progress: double progression

    Pick a repetition range, such as 8 to 12. Keep the same weight until you can do 12 good repetitions in every set, then add the smallest weight step you can and drop back to 8. Repeat.

    What a session looks like

    1. Warm up, 5 minutes. Brisk walking, marching or cycling, then a light practice set of your first exercises.
    2. Five to eight exercises covering legs, hips, a push, a pull and a carry or trunk exercise. The exercise library has them all.
    3. One to three sets of each, usually 8 to 12 repetitions, finishing with 1 to 3 in reserve. Rest 1 to 2 minutes between sets.
    4. Finish with balance, such as a single-leg stand, which helps prevent falls.
    5. Breathe. Breathe out during the effort rather than holding your breath, which avoids a spike in blood pressure and pelvic floor pressure ().

    Cardio, steps and strength together

    • Adults are advised to do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, a week as well as strength work, and to sit less.
    • For adults 60 and over, the benefit of more daily steps levelled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day.
    • Doing cardio and strength training together still builds strength and muscle, only slightly less than strength training alone, and the small interference was greater with running than with cycling.

    Coaching helps

    In adults over 65, supervised training improved strength and balance more than unsupervised training, and the authors suggest two of three weekly sessions be supervised. An , physiotherapist or qualified trainer can check your technique and progress you safely. Find help

      Joint pain and osteoarthritis

      Exercise is a core treatment for osteoarthritis, not something to avoid. In 54 trials of people with knee osteoarthritis, exercise reduced pain and improved function by an amount comparable with anti-inflammatory medicines, and the benefit lasted two to six months after treatment ended. Progressive resistance training also eased pain in people with osteoarthritis in the Cochrane review of older adults.

      A guide many physiotherapists use: some discomfort during exercise is usually fine if it stays mild and tolerable, settles within a day, and isn't building up from week to week. If it is worse than that, reduce the load or range, or swap the exercise, rather than stopping altogether.

      Squatting to a higher chair, shorter steps and pain-free ranges keep most leg training possible.

      Osteoporosis and low bone density

      An international expert panel strongly recommends a programme that includes resistance and balance training for people with , including after a spinal fracture, and advises against relying on aerobic exercise alone. In the Australian LIFTMOR trial, women around 65 with low bone mass did supervised, heavy lifting and impact training twice a week for 8 months. Spine bone density rose by 2.9% while it fell by 1.2% in the comparison group, every function test improved, and there was one minor adverse event, a back spasm.

      The key word is supervised. If you have osteoporosis, and especially if you have had a fracture of the spine, work with a physiotherapist or exercise physiologist to build up to heavier loads. Bones and balance

      Your pelvic floor

      The supports the bladder, bowel and uterus. Leaking when you cough, laugh or lift is common, but it isn't something you have to put up with.

      • Pelvic floor muscle training works. It can cure or improve stress urinary incontinence and other types of leaking.
      • Lifting isn't the enemy. In a survey of 3,934 women, those who lifted heavy weights for exercise had no more symptoms of than those who didn't lift. A survey shows a link, not cause and effect.
      • Breathe out with the effort, and gently lift your pelvic floor just before you lift or push, rather than holding your breath and bearing down.
      • See a pelvic health physiotherapist if you leak, feel heaviness, dragging or a bulge, or have pelvic pain. You don't need a referral. In Australia the National Continence Helpline (1800 33 00 66) is free. More help

        Bones and balance

        Bone is living tissue that responds to load, much as muscle does. Strength training helps bone a little, and balance training lowers the chance of the fall that breaks it.

        What happens to bone

        Bone loss speeds up from about a year before the final period. Over 10 years, women in the SWAN study lost on average 10.6% of their spine and 9.1% at the hip. When density falls below the young adult average it is called , and when it falls further, . A measures it; ask your GP whether you should have one.

        What exercise does for bone

        • In trials with 5,300 postmenopausal women, exercise had a real but small effect on bone density at the spine and hip.
        • Heavier, supervised training may do more. In the LIFTMOR trial, women around 65 with low bone mass who lifted heavy loads (5 sets of 5 at over 85% of their maximum) plus impact training for 8 months gained 2.9% spine bone density while the comparison group lost 1.2%. They also grew slightly taller and improved every function test.
        • For people with osteoporosis or a spinal fracture, an international panel strongly recommends a programme with resistance and balance training, and not aerobic training alone.

        Heavy lifting in LIFTMOR was closely supervised, and only women without conditions or medicines affecting bone took part. If you have osteoporosis, build up to heavier loads with a physiotherapist or exercise physiologist.

        Balance: the best-proven way to prevent falls

        A Cochrane review of 108 trials with more than 23,000 people over 60 found exercise reduced the rate of falls by 23%. Balance and functional exercises, such as standing on one leg, stepping and getting up from a chair, did the heavy lifting: they cut falls by 24%, and programmes that added resistance training probably cut them by 34%. Tai chi may also help.

        The same review was uncertain whether resistance training on its own, walking or dance prevents falls, so make balance part of every week.

        • Practise most days, for a few minutes: while the kettle boils, or while brushing your teeth.
        • Always have something sturdy within reach. See the single-leg stand
        • Harder versions: turn your head slowly, reach in different directions, or stand on a cushion.

        Calcium and vitamin D

        • Women 51 and over are advised to get 1,300 mg of calcium a day, ideally from food such as milk, yoghurt, cheese, calcium-set tofu and canned fish with bones.
        • Vitamin D supplements didn't prevent fractures or falls, or meaningfully change bone density, across 81 trials in adults, at high or low doses. If a blood test shows you're low in vitamin D, your doctor may still recommend one.
        • If you have osteoporosis, bone-protecting medicines can lower fracture risk; ask your GP. Exercise works alongside them, not instead of them. Healthy Bones Australia and other help

        Spread it across your meals

        • In laboratory studies, healthy men around 71 needed about 0.4 g of protein per kg of body weight in a single meal to switch on fully, compared with 0.24 g per kg in men around 22. For a 70 kg woman, 0.4 g per kg is about 28 g. Women weren't studied.

        • If most of your protein comes at dinner, adding a protein food to breakfast is often the easiest change to make.
        • Eating protein right before or after a workout made no difference once total daily protein was taken into account. Hitting your daily total matters more than timing.

        Which protein?

        • Leucine-rich foods help. In 22 women aged about 69, a small dose of milk protein with added built muscle protein as well as a larger dose of whey. Dairy, meat, fish, eggs and soy are good sources.
        • Plant protein works too. Soy and animal protein supplements gave similar gains in strength and lean mass with resistance training. If you eat mostly plants, combine legumes, soy foods, nuts and seeds across the day.
        • Higher protein is kind to healthy kidneys. Diets with 1.5 g per kg a day or more didn't harm kidney function in adults without kidney disease. If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor or dietitian first.

        Eat

        Eating for muscle

        There's no special diet for muscle. Enough food, enough protein and a pattern built on whole foods give your training something to work with. If you're losing weight, protecting muscle takes deliberate effort.

        An everyday pattern

        • Build each meal around a protein food, then add vegetables, fruit, wholegrains and some healthy fat such as olive oil, nuts or avocado. How much protein
        • Include dairy or calcium-rich alternatives to help reach 1,300 mg of calcium a day after 50.
        • A Mediterranean-style pattern (plenty of vegetables, legumes, wholegrains, fish and olive oil) was linked with a lower chance of becoming frail in older people. These were observational studies, so they show a link, not proof.
        • Eat enough on training days. Very low energy intake makes it hard to build muscle, so if you're not trying to lose weight, don't undereat.

        Losing weight without losing muscle

        When you lose weight, some of what you lose is : muscle, along with water, organ tissue and bone. How much depends on how you lose it.

        • Another analysis of 20 trials found lean mass made up 25% to 39% of the weight lost on semaglutide, tirzepatide or liraglutide, and about 26% with intensive lifestyle programmes. Lifestyle programmes that included resistance training lost the least, about 18%.
        • In about two thirds of the medicine trials in a third review, losses of muscle-related tissue were larger than the 25% usually expected, and the same happened in nearly half of the non-medicine programmes.
        • In 160 adults over 65 with obesity, dieting for 6 months with combined aerobic and resistance exercise improved physical function the most and best preserved muscle and bone, compared with either type of exercise alone.

        If you take a GLP-1 medicine

        • Keep strength training two or three times a week. In these studies, weight-loss programmes that included exercise, especially resistance training, lost the smallest share of lean mass.
        • Put protein first. When your appetite is small, eat the protein part of your meal before the rest.
        • Get help from an Accredited Practising Dietitian, who can make sure you're eating enough protein and nutrients on a smaller intake. Find one
        • Track strength, not just weight. Repeat the chair-stand test every couple of months: holding or improving your score is a good sign.

        • Across 22 trials with 721 women and men averaging 57 to 70, creatine during training added about 1.4 kg more lean tissue and more chest and leg press strength.
        • In a 2-year trial in 237 postmenopausal women, creatine did not improve hip or spine bone density, although some measures of hip bone shape improved.
        • Dose and safety. Creatine monohydrate is the most studied form; 3 to 5 g a day is a common dose, and use for up to 5 years appeared safe in healthy people. It didn't damage kidney function in trials, but it can raise blood creatinine, a marker doctors use to estimate kidney function. Tell your doctor if you take it, and check first if you have kidney disease.
        • Part of the extra lean mass may be water held in muscle, so a small rise on the scales is expected.

        The rest

        Protein powdersConvenient, not essential

        Adding protein during training increased strength by about 2.5 kg on a one-repetition test and lean mass by 0.3 kg on average, with smaller benefits in older people and no extra gain above about 1.6 g per kg a day. If you can reach your target with food, you don't need a powder; if appetite is small, a milk-based or soy shake can help. Protein planner

        Vitamin DTreat a deficiency, don't expect more

        Across 30 trials with 5,615 people, vitamin D had a small effect on strength, mostly in people who were low in it (under 30 nmol/L) or 65 and over, and no effect on muscle mass or power. It didn't prevent falls or fractures across 81 trials.

        Fish oilInteresting, unproven

        In one trial of 60 healthy adults aged 60 to 85, six months of fish oil capsules increased thigh muscle volume by 3.6% and improved grip and lifting strength compared with corn oil. One small trial, with only 44 people finishing, isn't enough to recommend it for muscle.

        CollagenOne trial, in men

        In 53 men around 72 with sarcopenia, 15 g a day of collagen peptides alongside 12 weeks of training added more fat-free mass and strength than a placebo. It hasn't been tested the same way in women.

        Supplements aren't checked as strictly as medicines, and some interact with prescriptions. Tell your doctor or pharmacist what you take, especially if you have kidney disease or take several medicines.

        Check and support

        Self-checks

        Simple tests that clinicians use to check leg strength, grip and the risk of sarcopenia. Repeat them every couple of months to see your training at work. Results are saved on this device only.

        Before you test: use a sturdy chair without wheels, pushed against a wall. Stop if you feel pain, dizziness or chest discomfort. If you've been told not to exercise, or you answered yes in the check-in, ask your doctor first.

        30-second chair stand

        Counts how many times you can stand up fully and sit back down in 30 seconds. It is a reliable measure of leg strength, and scores fall with age and with inactivity.

        1. Sit in the middle of a chair with a seat about 43 cm (17 inches) high, back straight, feet flat and hip-width apart.
        2. Cross your arms over your chest.
        3. When the timer says go, stand all the way up, then sit all the way down. Repeat as many times as you can until it says stop.
        4. Count each full stand. If you're more than halfway up when time runs out, it counts. If you need your arms to stand, stop: note it, and talk to your doctor or physiotherapist.

            Evidence library

            Every claim in Hale links to its sources. Here is how they were chosen and graded, what the evidence does not support, and the full list with the people each study included.

            How claims are graded

            Strong

            Consistent results from several good randomised trials, or a high-quality systematic review or of them.

            Moderate

            Good evidence with some limits: fewer or smaller trials, mixed results, or large that show links rather than cause and effect.

            Weak

            Early evidence: a single trial, small or short studies, laboratory measures, or studies in a different group (for example, men only).

            Not supported

            Tested, and not shown to help: either it didn't work, or the evidence was too uncertain to say it does.

            Some sources, such as national guidelines, definitions and screening tools, carry no grade of their own: they are cited for what they recommend or define. Practical advice that rests on professional agreement rather than trials is labelled clinical consensus.

            How sources were chosen and checked

            • Systematic reviews, meta-analyses and randomised trials were preferred, then large cohort studies, then smaller trials where nothing better exists.
            • Each journal source was checked on PubMed on 17 November 2026, and its PMID and DOI link to the record. Numbers in the text were checked against each study's abstract.
            • The people studied are listed with every source. Many studies included men or younger adults; where that matters, the text says so.
            • Guidelines and service details were checked on the same date, using official websites and documents wherever they could be reached.

            What the evidence doesn't support

            • Menopausal hormone therapy to build or keep muscle mass.
            • Exercise in general as a proven treatment for hot flushes, although strength training looks promising.
            • Timing protein around workouts, once daily total protein is enough.
            • Vitamin D supplements to prevent falls or fractures, or to build muscle mass.
            • Creatine to improve bone density.
            • Resistance training on its own, walking or dance as proven ways to prevent falls: balance and functional exercise are what reduce falls.
            • The idea that women must train only with light weights, or that heavy lifting causes prolapse.

            Who does what

            GP (family doctor)Your starting point

            Checks your heart, blood pressure, blood sugar and medicines before you step up exercise, arranges a bone density scan if you need one, discusses menopause treatment, and can refer you to allied health professionals.

            Accredited Exercise PhysiologistExercise for health conditions

            A university-qualified allied health professional who designs and supervises exercise for people with conditions such as osteoporosis, arthritis, diabetes and heart disease. Supervised training improved strength and balance more than training alone.

            PhysiotherapistPain, injury and the pelvic floor

            Assesses and treats joint and muscle pain and injury, and prescribes exercise. Pelvic health physiotherapists specialise in leaking, prolapse and pelvic pain.

            Accredited Practising DietitianFood, protein and weight

            Helps you meet your protein and calcium needs, lose weight while protecting muscle, and eat well on GLP-1 medicines or with kidney disease.

            Menopause doctorSymptoms and hormone therapy

            A GP or specialist with a special interest in menopause can talk through symptoms, hormone therapy and bone health.
